\section{A feature-based probe and its decision target}
\label{sec:cpm_results}

We test whether a feature-based probe improves graph-versus-MLP decisions using a training-label-only adaptation of the Gaussian Naive Bayes version of CPM~\citep{luan2023whendo}. The probe compares classification on raw and one-hop-aggregated features through 100 paired inner holdouts per dataset, seed, and input condition. We computed all 220 metric records before opening the saved candidate outcomes. Fixed rules and the matched LODO procedure in Section~\ref{sec:matched_audit} use these scores. Appendix~\ref{app:cpm} specifies the operator, sampling, adaptations, and all 16 portfolio/input/budget configurations.

The fixed 0.5 rule has lower descriptive regret than both always-graph and always-MLP for GCN, GAT, and GCN+GAT under every input/budget configuration, but higher regret for the full portfolio (Appendix~\ref{app:cpm}). For example, under CS/24 its regret is 0.172 points against 7.468 for always-GCN and 4.934 for always-MLP, whereas the full-portfolio comparison is 1.561 against 0.528. These comparisons have different portfolio oracles. In the full portfolio, the fixed rule captures all available benefit but introduces greater harm. Its decisions coincide with the zero-gap rule and the two-cutoff rule, which never abstains on these records.

Roman-empire accounts for 78.7--86.5\% of full-portfolio CPM-.5 regret (Table~\ref{tab:cpm_influence}). Recomputing regret after every single-dataset omission preserves all actions and gives the remaining datasets equal weight. Omitting Roman-empire reverses the comparison with always-graph in N/24, CS/4, and CS/24; N/4 still favors always-graph. Appendix~\ref{app:source_sensitivity} reports every omission and each dataset's benefit and harm in all four full-portfolio configurations. The full eleven-dataset set remains primary.

CPM-L chooses MLP only on Roman-empire in all four full-portfolio configurations, producing harm without benefit. Gap-L improves over always-graph only under N/24, where its 0.068-point net gain comes entirely from Cornell. The probe is useful for the restricted choices, while its performance on the full portfolio depends on calibration and the model-selection target.

Score resolution also constrains calibration. Under each input condition, 30 of 110 scores equal floating-point 1.0, leaving only 81 distinct values. Different probe gaps can therefore map to the same cutoff value. Appendix~\ref{app:cpm} reports dataset ranges and endpoint counts. The records show both target mismatch and score compression, without separating their contributions to calibrated loss.

In exact arithmetic, GNB with the specified variance floor is invariant to a common nonzero rescaling and feature shift. A row-stochastic graph operator preserves that relation, which holds between N and CS. On the actual float32 inputs, the largest CPM-score difference is $2.78\times10^{-4}$ and all fixed decisions remain unchanged. The trained candidates' outcomes change, separating probe stability from the neural candidates' optimization response.

Equal-class fit quotas leave two classes absent from every Cornell and Wisconsin inner holdout. Their scores therefore describe performance on the remaining holdout classes under that sampling distribution. Appendix~\ref{app:cpm} reports actual fit fractions and per-class supports reconstructed from bound labels, with unchanged partition hashes and no refitting.

\section{Introduction}

Choosing between a graph neural network (GNN) and a feature-only multilayer perceptron (MLP) is a model-selection decision. Graph structure can improve node classification, but its value depends on the models available and how they are trained~\citep{kipf2017gcn,velivckovic2018gat,hamilton2017graphsage}. A diagnostic intended to guide this choice must therefore be evaluated through the predictions it selects. A statistic can describe a graph accurately yet recommend a model that loses accuracy relative to a simple reference policy.

Research on homophily and heterophily has developed class-conditional, feature, structural, and neighborhood-distribution measures to characterize graph learning~\citep{luan2023whendo,platonov2023characterizing,zheng2024trihom,pmlr-v235-wang24u}. Learned selectors use historical performance to rank graph models on new datasets~\citep{park2023metagl,park2023glemos}. We use simple threshold rules as transparent case studies of a complementary question: how do fallback and the candidate portfolio determine the accuracy cost of fixed graph-versus-MLP decisions? Holding those decisions fixed makes their changing comparison with reference policies directly inspectable.

We address this question with an offline paired decision evaluation on 11 datasets and ten seeds. Nine diagnostic and reference policies use the same trained outcomes, validation-selection rules, and train-label information boundary. We measure oracle-portfolio regret, the accuracy lost relative to the better selected graph or MLP model, alongside selection accuracy and abstention coverage. The primary protocol was fixed before generating its benchmark records. Its historical Combined rule has an exploratory origin: the thresholds were chosen after inspecting legacy aggregates. The full graph action searches six architectures with four trials each, while MLP receives four trials; the study evaluates this specified budget allocation.

The results connect diagnostic utility to the complete decision setting. Combined with its declared MLP fallback incurs 7.46 percentage points of regret, versus 0.26 for always-graph. This loss is highly concentrated: 95.1\% comes from Roman-empire, Amazon-ratings, Squirrel, and Chameleon. Separate training diagnostics document input-related MLP degradation on the first two datasets; the latter two use original versions with known duplicate-node concerns. The 75.9\% of loss occurring after abstention is therefore a decomposition of the recorded policy outcomes, not an isolated effect of fallback. Changing the graph candidates while retaining the diagnostic's decisions reverses the relative ordering for GCN and GAT. In the frozen records, those descriptive advantages occur in only three of the 63 candidate subsets and disappear when Cornell and Wisconsin are jointly excluded. A later post-hoc retraining of all 11 datasets under a training-fitted input reparameterization, and a 24-trial search for the same multilayer perceptron architecture, change the feature-only baseline and several regret values without reversing the full-portfolio comparison with always-graph (Sections~\ref{sec:input_robustness}--\ref{sec:mlp_budget}).

The paper makes three contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Diagnostic loss and portfolio sensitivity.} We locate decision costs in explicit actions and fallback, and show how fixed decisions change their comparison with reference policies across candidate portfolios and dataset compositions.
    \item \textbf{An auditable decision-evaluation protocol.} We combine established principles of information control, validation selection, full-policy scoring, and dataset-level inference in a record-based audit of regret, coverage, and available improvement over reference policies.
    \item \textbf{Auxiliary evidence of graph signal.} A degree-preserving edge intervention measures GCN sensitivity to non-degree edge organization on three citation networks, distinguishing the use of graph structure from the value of a model-selection rule.
\end{enumerate}

The protocol serves benchmark builders, diagnostic designers, and applied researchers auditing a proposed selector before reuse. It identifies whether losses arise from explicit choices, fallback, or changes in the available models. The primary comparison and post-hoc sensitivities retain distinct inferential roles.

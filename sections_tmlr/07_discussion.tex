\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{The Diagnostic and the Portfolio Measure Different Quantities}
\label{sec:mismatch}

The negative result admits a structural explanation rather than a threshold-tuning one. Table~\ref{tab:winning_architectures} reports the validation-selected graph architecture on every unit. Heterophily-aware architectures---H2GCN, LINKX, and GPR-GNN---win 95 of the 110 units. Their dominance is not confined to the heterophilous regime: they also win all ten units on PubMed and all ten on Coauthor-CS, whose training-label homophily exceeds 0.80. The graph action is therefore not a proxy for GCN-style message passing. It is a portfolio whose realized advantage is largely supplied by architectures built to remain effective when one-hop homophily is low.

An edge-homophily threshold grants the graph action exactly on the high-homophily datasets. The portfolio's advantage, however, is largest elsewhere. The four datasets that carry 95.1\% of the combined rule's regret---Roman-empire, Squirrel, Amazon-ratings, and Chameleon---all have training-label homophily below the frozen 0.55 threshold, so the rule declines the graph action on all 40 of those units. Thirty-five of those 40 units are won by a heterophily-aware architecture, with mean graph-minus-MLP gaps between 15.9 and 23.7 percentage points. The remaining seven datasets together account for 4.9\% of the loss.

The diagnostic and the decision target are thus anti-correlated over precisely the regime that dominates the loss. This is why no threshold on the statistic can repair the rule. Lowering the threshold to admit the four costly datasets would also admit Actor, Cornell, and Wisconsin, where MLP is the target on 21 of 30 units; raising it forfeits the datasets the rule already handles. The statistic orders datasets by one-hop label mixing, whereas the decision requires knowing whether \emph{any} member of a heterophily-aware portfolio beats a tuned MLP---a quantity that one-hop mixing does not order.

Section~\ref{sec:equal_budget} tests this account by varying the pairing directly rather than inferring it from which architecture won. Restricting the graph action to one architecture at matched budget orders the combined rule's mean regret as the account predicts: lowest against the GCN-style actions that one-hop homophily was built to describe (1.74 points for GCN, 2.08 for GAT) and highest against the heterophily-aware LINKX (7.99). The unrestricted portfolio behaves like the latter because heterophily-aware architectures supply most of its realized advantage. The mismatch is therefore not an artifact of the particular thresholds frozen here; it moves when the portfolio moves.

That same analysis bounds what we may conclude. Against a GCN-only or GAT-only action the combined rule attains lower mean full-set regret than always-graph, so the diagnostics are not useless in general. What does not appear at matched budget is a consistent dataset-level direction: the rule wins on the small low-homophily datasets where a GCN-style model genuinely loses to the MLP, and loses on Chameleon and Squirrel where a GCN-style model wins despite low homophily. Our claim is accordingly that these diagnostics provide no \emph{stable} incremental decision value for the portfolios studied.

We state the consequence narrowly but generally: any selection rule built on a scalar summary of one-hop label mixing inherits this mismatch whenever the candidate portfolio contains heterophily-aware models. The mismatch is a property of the pairing between statistic and portfolio, not of the particular thresholds frozen here. It does not imply that richer diagnostics, class-conditional characterizations, or learned selectors must fail; it does imply that a diagnostic must be validated against the portfolio it is meant to govern rather than against a single architecture. The practical sting is that the mismatch is worst in the setting where a cheap diagnostic would be most valuable: a heterophily-aware portfolio is the expensive one to tune, and it is the one a homophily threshold describes least well.

\subsection{What the Negative Result Establishes}

The prospective benchmark evaluates diagnostics as actions rather than as post-hoc correlates. Under the frozen 11-dataset protocol, the combined rule and the four structure-based variants do not provide stable incremental value over an always-graph policy. This conclusion is bounded to the evaluated statistics, thresholds, model portfolio, accuracy target, and one-percentage-point margin. It does not imply that every possible graph diagnostic must fail or that cross-dataset learned selectors are impossible.

The comparison also shows why trivial policies belong in diagnostic studies. Graph is the target in 89 of 110 units, making always-graph a strong reference before any structural statistic is consulted. A diagnostic that predicts the majority action on many units can appear accurate while still adding no decision value. Oracle-portfolio regret exposes the cost of the remaining errors, and coverage prevents abstention from receiving favorable treatment. These quantities should accompany correlation whenever a graph statistic is proposed for operational selection.

\subsection{Graph Utility Is Not a Scalar Property}

The degree-preserving intervention changes the interpretation of the negative result. Large paired losses after randomization show that useful edge organization is present in Cora, CiteSeer, and PubMed even though homophily, degree, and the two-hop statistic do not yield a reliable general selector. The apparent tension is resolved if graph utility depends on interactions that low-dimensional summaries discard: class-specific mixing, feature alignment, motifs, long-range paths, architecture, and optimization can affect performance jointly. The fixed-degree analysis in Section~\ref{sec:snr} illustrates one such interaction in a controlled surrogate, but it is not a model-selection theorem.

Validation selection has the lowest descriptive regret in the benchmark, although its advantage over the combined rule does not remain significant after the predeclared Holm correction. Unlike a cheap structural rule, validation selection requires training both model families. It therefore serves as a performance reference rather than a cost-free diagnostic. A useful future selector must be evaluated against both extremes: the trivial family policy that is strong under the observed target distribution and the validation policy that spends the full training budget.

\subsection{Limitations and External Validity}

The study covers 11 public node-classification datasets, ten seeds, one stratified split design, seven architectures, four trials per architecture, and test accuracy as the outcome. The thresholds are intentionally simple and frozen; they are not optimized for each dataset. The graph action selects from six architectures (24 trials in total), whereas the feature-only action uses one MLP architecture (four trials in total). The findings therefore describe these asymmetric operational portfolios and do not establish an equal-total-compute comparison; the wider graph search may partly contribute to graph being the target in 89 units. These choices make the negative result auditable but limit generalization to inductive deployment, graph-level tasks, dynamic or heterogeneous graphs, different model portfolios, and cost-sensitive objectives. The eligibility amendment also excludes Texas because its singleton class cannot support the fixed three-way split, so the benchmark does not characterize that small-data regime.

The intervention adds a different limitation. It evaluates GCN on three citation networks, leaving only three independent dataset clusters for inference. Degree preservation does not preserve connected components, homophily, motifs, spectral structure, or path statistics. The intervention therefore establishes sensitivity to non-degree edge organization, not a causal effect of any one structural attribute.

\subsection{Implications for Future Work}

The next step is not another hand-tuned threshold over the same summaries. More promising directions include learned representations of structural utility, uncertainty-aware abstention, explicit training-cost objectives, sensitivity analyses with equal total compute, and evaluation on held-out or external prospective datasets. Such work should retain the safeguards used here: decision-time provenance, explicit portfolio budgets, test-once accounting, trivial policies, regret and coverage, dataset-level inference, and immutable per-unit records.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper asks whether transparent graph diagnostics can decide when node classification should use a tuned graph-model portfolio rather than a tuned feature-only MLP. In a frozen prospective benchmark spanning 11 datasets, ten seeds, 770 model records, and 110 diagnostic records, the historical combined rule reaches 55.5\% selection accuracy and 7.46 percentage points of full-set regret. Always-graph reaches 80.9\% and 0.26 points, while validation selection reaches 91.8\% and 0.22 points. The estimand compares fixed asymmetric portfolios---six graph architectures with 24 total trials against one MLP architecture with four---rather than equal-total-compute families.

The paper's main result is the explanation of that failure. The diagnostics and the decision target measure different quantities and are anti-correlated over the regime that dominates the loss. Heterophily-aware architectures win 95 of the 110 units, including every unit on the two most homophilous datasets, so the graph action is not a proxy for GCN-style message passing. An edge-homophily threshold grants the graph action only where homophily is high, whereas 95.1\% of the combined rule's regret falls on four datasets below that threshold, where the rule declines the graph action on all 40 units and 35 of them are won by a heterophily-aware architecture. No threshold on a scalar one-hop summary repairs this, and the diagnosis extends to any such rule paired with a heterophily-aware portfolio.

An equal-total-compute analysis, recomputed from the same frozen records without retraining, both confirms the account and bounds it. Restricting the graph action to a single architecture orders the combined rule's mean regret as the mismatch predicts---1.74 points against GCN and 2.08 against GAT, rising to 7.99 against LINKX---so diagnostic utility depends on the pairing rather than on the statistic alone. It also shows that the rule is not useless in general: against a GCN-style action at matched budget it attains lower mean regret than always-graph. What is absent at matched budget is a consistent dataset-level direction, so our claim is that these diagnostics provide no \emph{stable} incremental decision value for the portfolios studied, not that they carry no information under any portfolio.

Two further results bound the interpretation. Degree-preserving randomization lowers GCN accuracy by 45.5, 29.9, and 17.3 points on Cora, CiteSeer, and PubMed, so diagnostic failure is not evidence that edge organization is uninformative; because the intervention changes several forms of edge organization and covers only three dataset clusters, it does not isolate homophily or support a universal causal claim. Separately, the predeclared sign-flip test could not have rejected at $\alpha=0.05$ under any realization of the data at this dataset count, which we report as a design limitation rather than as a small measured effect; the primary comparison is descriptive and does not rest on it.

Beyond these findings, the paper delivers the evaluation protocol itself: frozen decision-time information boundaries, matched trivial and validation-based policies, explicit abstention and coverage, oracle-portfolio regret, test-once accounting, dataset-level inference, immutable per-unit provenance, and a deterministic audit binding every reported value to the frozen artifacts. Richer or learned representations of graph utility can be tested against the same protocol. The evidence here argues against treating a single structural statistic as a general graph-vs-MLP selection rule, and in favor of validating any candidate diagnostic against the model portfolio it is meant to govern.

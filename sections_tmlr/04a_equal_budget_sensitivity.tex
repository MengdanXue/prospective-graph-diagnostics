\section{Equal-Total-Compute Sensitivity}
\label{sec:equal_budget}

\subsection{Why the Asymmetry Matters}

The frozen estimand compares operational portfolios rather than equal-compute families: the graph action selects among six architectures with 24 trials in total, while the feature-only action contains one MLP with four. Because a wider search can only improve the selected graph model, the asymmetry inflates both the rate at which graph is the target action and the strength of the always-graph reference. Any conclusion drawn against that reference therefore needs a matched-budget check.

We supply one without retraining. The frozen artifact stores an independent record for each of the seven architectures on each of the 110 units, so restricting the graph action to a single architecture yields a four-trial-against-four-trial comparison that is recomputable from the immutable records. The recomputation applies the evaluator's own portfolio rule---validation accuracy descending, then model identifier ascending---and the analysis script refuses to emit a summary unless the unrestricted six-architecture portfolio first reproduces the published values. It does: 89 graph targets, 0.26 points of always-graph regret, 7.46 for the combined rule, and 0.22 for validation selection.

The diagnostic policies are unaffected by this restriction. Edge homophily and MLP validation accuracy do not depend on which graph architectures are available, so each rule's action is reused unchanged; only the realized outcomes, the decision target, and validation selection are recomputed.

Table~\ref{tab:equal_budget} reports the outcome. Three findings follow, and we state the second against our own interest.

First, the asymmetry is real and consequential. The graph target rate falls from 89 of 110 units to between 41 (GAT) and 78 (H2GCN) once the graph action is a single architecture, and always-graph weakens accordingly, reaching 5.29 points of regret for GCN and 6.35 for GAT against 0.26 for the unrestricted portfolio. Readers should not transfer the unrestricted always-graph baseline to an equal-compute setting.

Second, the combined rule's mean regret improves sharply under two of the restrictions: 1.74 points for a GCN-only action and 2.08 for GAT, against 7.46 for the full portfolio. On mean full-set regret alone, the rule beats always-graph in both of those settings. A conclusion phrased as ``these diagnostics are useless'' would therefore be too strong, and we do not make it.

Third, the dataset-level picture---the preregistered inferential unit---does not show a consistent advantage even where the mean flips. Under GCN the datasets split three wins, three losses, and five ties; under GAT, four, two, and five. The combined rule wins by wide margins on Wisconsin, Cornell, and Actor, where low homophily correctly predicts that a GCN-style model will not beat the MLP, and loses on Chameleon and Squirrel, where a GCN-style model beats the MLP despite low homophily. With at most six datasets carrying a nonzero paired difference, the attainable two-sided sign-flip $p$-value floor is $0.03125$ before any correction, so these comparisons cannot resolve the direction either. The claim that the evaluated diagnostics provide no \emph{stable} incremental decision value survives at matched budget; the stronger claim that they provide no value under any portfolio does not, and was never made.

\subsection{Interpretation}

The pattern across Table~\ref{tab:equal_budget} is ordered, not arbitrary, and it is the pattern the structural-mismatch account of Section~\ref{sec:mismatch} predicts. The combined rule's mean regret is lowest exactly where the graph action is a GCN-style architecture whose benefit one-hop homophily was designed to describe (1.74 for GCN, 2.08 for GAT), and highest where the graph action is a heterophily-aware architecture that remains effective at low homophily (7.99 for LINKX). The unrestricted portfolio behaves like the latter case because heterophily-aware architectures supply most of its realized advantage.

Diagnostic utility is therefore a property of the pairing between a statistic and the portfolio it governs, not of the statistic alone. This sharpens rather than softens the operational conclusion. A practitioner who has already committed to a heterophily-aware portfolio---the setting in which a cheap pre-training decision is most valuable, because that portfolio is the expensive one to tune---is precisely the practitioner for whom a homophily threshold is least informative.
